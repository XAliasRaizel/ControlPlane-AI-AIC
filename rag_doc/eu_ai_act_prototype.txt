EU AI Act — Prototype Regulatory Corpus (Summary Form)
========================================================
NOTE: Plain-language summary for Policy RAG demo/retrieval purposes, not
verbatim legal text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. Verified against current
public reporting as of August 2026; confirm against the official Official
Journal text and any further amendments before real use. This regulation's
timeline has already changed once via the Digital Omnibus (see below), so
treat every date in this document as subject to revision.

Overview and phased timeline
----------------------------------------------------------------
The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies obligations
in stages rather than all at once:
  - 2 February 2025: the Article 5 prohibited-practices regime became
    enforceable (certain social scoring, manipulative or exploitative
    systems, some biometric categorisation), alongside the Article 4 AI
    literacy duty requiring providers and deployers to ensure staff
    operating AI systems have a sufficient level of AI literacy.
  - 2 August 2025: obligations for providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI)
    models took effect.
  - 2 August 2026: Article 50 transparency obligations became enforceable
    -- disclosure that a user is interacting with an AI system, labelling
    of synthetic/deepfake content, and marking of AI-generated content.
    This date has already passed as of this document's writing and these
    obligations are now in effect and enforceable by national market
    surveillance authorities.
  - High-risk AI system obligations (Annex III: biometrics, critical
    infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law
    enforcement, migration, and administration of justice) were originally
    also scheduled for 2 August 2026. This has been formally deferred --
    see "Digital Omnibus" below, now enacted law rather than a pending
    proposal.

Digital Omnibus on AI — now enacted, not merely proposed
----------------------------------------------------------------
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the "Digital Omnibus on AI," was published in
the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July
2026 -- six days before the AI Act's original 2 August 2026 high-risk
deadline. This is confirmed, enacted law, not a political agreement still
awaiting formal adoption. It defers standalone high-risk (Annex III)
obligations from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and high-risk AI
embedded in products already covered by separate EU product-safety law
(Annex I) to 2 August 2028. Three categories of obligation were
deliberately left untouched by this deferral and remain fully enforceable
on their original schedule: the Article 5 prohibited-practices regime
(since 2 February 2025), the Article 4 AI literacy duty (also since 2
February 2025), and the Article 50 transparency and content-labelling
obligations (since 2 August 2026, now in effect). GPAI provider
obligations under Article 53, in force since 2 August 2025, are likewise
unaffected. The Omnibus also introduced a narrow additional prohibited
practice into Article 5 covering AI-generated non-consensual intimate
imagery and child sexual abuse material. The overall architecture of the
Act -- its risk-based classification, conformity assessment regime, and
the AI Office's oversight role -- is unchanged; this is a timing
adjustment to the heaviest compliance track, not a substantive rollback.

The four-tier risk classification
----------------------------------------------------------------
The Act classifies AI systems into four risk tiers, and the obligations
that apply to a given system flow entirely from which tier it falls into:
unacceptable risk (Article 5 prohibited practices -- these systems may
not be placed on the market at all, regardless of context); high risk
(Annex III use-case list, or AI embedded in products already subject to
EU product-safety law under Annex I -- subject to the risk-management,
documentation, and human-oversight regime described below, now on the
deferred timeline); limited risk (primarily the Article 50 transparency
obligations -- chatbots, emotion-recognition systems, and synthetic-
content generators, which must disclose their AI nature but face no
broader compliance regime); and minimal risk (the large majority of AI
applications, subject to no AI Act-specific obligations beyond voluntary
codes of conduct). Correctly classifying which tier a given AI use case
falls into is the foundational step every other AI Act obligation depends
on -- a governance system that already tags applications by criticality
and use case (loan-decision, hiring-decision, customer-support) is doing
work that maps directly onto this classification exercise, not a
separate one.

High-risk obligations (Annex III) — deferred, not removed
----------------------------------------------------------------
Annex III lists the specific high-risk use-case categories: biometric
identification and categorisation; management of critical infrastructure;
education and vocational training (e.g. automated exam scoring or
admissions); employment, worker management, and access to self-employment
(e.g. automated CV screening or performance evaluation); access to
essential private and public services including credit scoring and
insurance risk assessment; law enforcement; migration, asylum, and border
control; and administration of justice and democratic processes. For
systems in these categories, the Act requires (once the deferred deadline
arrives): a risk-management system spanning the AI system's lifecycle;
data governance measures for training/validation/testing data; technical
documentation and automatic logging capabilities; transparency
information for deployers; human oversight measures enabling a person to
understand the system's output and intervene or halt it; and defined
levels of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity. Employment decisions
and access to essential services are both explicitly listed categories --
this is the same reasoning ControlPlane already applies internally by
tagging loan-decision and hiring-decision applications as critical/
high-impact and routing them toward human review. That existing design
choice anticipates the Annex III risk-management, human-oversight, and
logging obligations even though the hard legal deadline for those
specific obligations has moved out to December 2027 -- building toward it
now is schedule flexibility, not an excuse to stop.

Article 14 — Human oversight
----------------------------------------------------------------
For high-risk systems, the Act requires human oversight measures
proportionate to the risks, appropriate to the context, and designed so
the overseeing person can: properly understand the system's capacities
and limitations, remain aware of "automation bias" (the tendency to
over-rely on automated output), correctly interpret the system's output,
decide not to use it or otherwise disregard, override, or reverse it in a
given instance, and intervene in or stop the system's operation. A
governance layer's HUMAN_REVIEW decision path, and its capture of what a
human reviewer actually decided when overriding a flagged output, is a
concrete operational instance of this obligation rather than an
independent design choice made for unrelated reasons.

Article 50 — Transparency obligations (in force now)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 50 requires that providers ensure AI systems intended to interact
directly with natural persons are designed so those persons are informed
they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the
context to a reasonably well-informed person. It further requires
labelling of AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content
that could appear to be authentic or truthful (synthetic/deepfake
content), and disclosure where text is AI-generated and published to
inform the public on matters of public interest, again with exceptions
where human review has taken place. This is already in force and
enforceable by national market surveillance authorities as of 2 August
2026, with penalties for non-compliance reaching up to EUR 15 million or
3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. For a system like
ControlPlane, this means any user-facing AI interaction (e.g. the HR or
customer-support chatbot sitting behind the governance gateway) should be
clearly disclosed as AI-driven, and any synthetic content it produces
that could be mistaken for authentic material should be identifiable as
AI-generated -- an obligation that is live today, not a future
compliance project.

Article 53 — Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models
----------------------------------------------------------------
Providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models -- the category most
foundation-model APIs an enterprise consumes fall into -- must maintain
technical documentation, provide information and documentation to
downstream providers integrating the model, put in place a policy to
respect EU copyright law, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of
the content used to train the model. GPAI models classified as posing a
"systemic risk" (broadly, based on the compute used to train them) face
additional obligations: model evaluation, adversarial testing, tracking
and reporting serious incidents, and ensuring adequate cybersecurity
protection. These obligations sit with the model provider, not with an
enterprise consuming that model via API -- but an enterprise governance
layer benefits from knowing which GPAI provider and model version sits
behind each AI application it governs, since that provider's own
documentation and incident disclosures are relevant context for the
enterprise's own risk assessment of that application.

Conformity assessment, documentation, and enforcement
----------------------------------------------------------------
High-risk AI systems must undergo a conformity assessment (self-assessed
for most Annex III categories, third-party assessed for a smaller subset)
before being placed on the market, and providers must register standalone
high-risk systems in an EU database. Article 99 sets out the Act's
general penalty structure: up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual
turnover for violations of the Article 5 prohibited-practices regime
(the most severe tier), up to EUR 15 million or 3% for most other
provider/deployer obligations including Article 50 transparency, and a
lower tier for supplying incorrect information to authorities. These
figures scale with company size for SMEs. National market surveillance
authorities and, for GPAI models, the EU AI Office, share enforcement
responsibility.

What this corpus intentionally does not cover
----------------------------------------------------------------
This summary does not cover the detailed conformity assessment procedure
steps, CE marking mechanics, the specific technical documentation format
required under Annex IV, or the AI Office's evolving guidance documents
and codes of practice -- those are compliance-process specifics for
providers placing high-risk systems on the market, not directly relevant
to an internal governance control plane's retrieval corpus. Given the
regulation's demonstrated rate of amendment (one substantive deferral via
the Digital Omnibus within two years of entry into force), any date or
figure in this document should be re-verified against the current
Official Journal text before being relied on for an actual compliance
decision.
