GDPR — Prototype Regulatory Corpus (Summary Form)
==================================================
NOTE ON THIS DOCUMENT: This is a prototype knowledge-base document for
ControlPlane.ai's Policy RAG. It is a plain-language SUMMARY of what each
article covers, written for retrieval and citation purposes in a demo
environment -- it is NOT the verbatim legal text of the Regulation, and
must not be used as a substitute for the official GDPR text (Regulation
(EU) 2016/679) or for legal advice. Replace with actual verified legal
source text (or a licensed regulatory database feed) before any real
compliance use.

Article 5 — Principles relating to processing of personal data
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 5 sets out the core principles that govern all processing of
personal data under GDPR. Personal data must be: processed lawfully,
fairly, and transparently; collected for specified, explicit, and
legitimate purposes and not further processed in a way incompatible with
those purposes (purpose limitation); adequate, relevant, and limited to
what is necessary for those purposes (data minimisation); accurate and
kept up to date; kept in a form permitting identification of the data
subject for no longer than necessary (storage limitation); and processed
with appropriate security, integrity, and confidentiality. The controller
is responsible for, and must be able to demonstrate compliance with,
these principles ("accountability"). For an enterprise AI governance
system, Article 5's data-minimisation and purpose-limitation principles
are directly relevant to any AI application that retrieves or surfaces
more personal data than the specific task requires.

Article 6 — Lawfulness of processing
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 6 requires that processing of personal data have a valid legal
basis. It lists six possible bases: the data subject has given consent;
processing is necessary for performance of a contract with the data
subject; processing is necessary to comply with a legal obligation;
processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data
subject or another person; processing is necessary for a task carried out
in the public interest; or processing is necessary for the legitimate
interests of the controller or a third party, provided those interests
are not overridden by the data subject's rights and freedoms. An AI
system that accesses employee or customer personal data (for example, an
HR copilot retrieving salary or leave records) must have an identifiable
Article 6 basis for that specific access, not merely a general basis for
holding the data in the first place.

Article 9 — Processing of special categories of personal data
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 9 imposes a general prohibition on processing "special category"
personal data -- data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political
opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership,
genetic data, biometric data used for identification, health data, or
data concerning a person's sex life or sexual orientation. Processing
this category of data is prohibited unless one of the specific exceptions
in Article 9(2) applies (for example: explicit consent, employment/social
security law obligations, vital interests, or substantial public
interest with appropriate safeguards). Health data is squarely within
this special category, which is why an AI system that could surface
medical or health-adjacent information carries materially higher risk
than one handling ordinary personal data, and why a governance system
should treat "health," "medical," or "diagnosis"-adjacent requests as a
distinct, higher-sensitivity class rather than folding them into generic
PII handling.

Article 12-14 — Transparency and the right to be informed
----------------------------------------------------------------
Articles 12 through 14 require that information provided to data subjects
about how their personal data is processed be concise, transparent,
intelligible, and in plain language -- not buried in dense legal text.
Article 13 covers information to give when data is collected directly
from the subject (identity of the controller, purposes, legal basis,
recipients, retention period, and the subject's rights); Article 14
covers the same when data is obtained from a third party rather than the
subject directly. For an AI system, this principle extends naturally to
disclosure that an automated or AI-assisted process is involved at all --
a user interacting with an AI-driven support or HR assistant has a
reasonable expectation of knowing that, which is also where GDPR's
transparency principle and the EU AI Act's Article 50 disclosure
obligation (see the EU AI Act corpus document) reinforce one another
rather than operating as two unrelated regimes.

Article 15 — Right of access
----------------------------------------------------------------
A data subject has the right to obtain confirmation of whether their
personal data is being processed, and if so, to access that data along
with supplementary information: the purposes of processing, the
categories of data involved, who it has been or will be disclosed to, the
retention period, and the source of the data if not collected directly
from them. In practice, this means an organization must be able to
locate and export everything a system holds about a specific person on
request, within one month (extendable to three for complex requests). An
AI governance platform that logs prompts, responses, and decisions
touching a specific individual's data should be structured so those
records can actually be retrieved by subject identity, not just by
request ID -- otherwise responding to an Article 15 request becomes a
manual, ad hoc search.

Article 17 — Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten")
----------------------------------------------------------------
A data subject can require a controller to erase their personal data
without undue delay where, among other grounds, the data is no longer
necessary for the purpose it was collected for, consent is withdrawn and
there is no other legal basis, the data was unlawfully processed, or the
subject objects and there are no overriding legitimate grounds. This
right is not absolute -- it does not apply where processing is necessary
for compliance with a legal obligation, for the exercise or defense of
legal claims, or for other specified exceptions. For a system like
ControlPlane, this has a direct operational consequence: audit logs
retained for compliance/accountability purposes generally fall under a
legal-obligation exception and are not simply deleted on request, but any
underlying personal data an AI application itself stored outside that
compliance record (for example, in a vector store or cache) is squarely
within scope of an erasure request and must be locatable and removable.

Article 22 — Automated individual decision-making, including profiling
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 22 gives individuals the right not to be subject to a decision
based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces
legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affects them --
for example, an automated rejection of a credit or loan application, or
an automated hiring decision, with no meaningful human involvement.
Exceptions apply where the decision is necessary for a contract, is
authorised by law with suitable safeguards, or is based on explicit
consent; even then, the data subject retains rights to obtain human
intervention, to express their point of view, and to contest the
decision. This is the direct regulatory basis for keeping a genuine
human-in-the-loop review path for high-impact automated decisions (loan
approvals, hiring recommendations) rather than allowing an AI system to
be the sole, unreviewable decision-maker in those categories -- which is
exactly the role of ControlPlane's HUMAN_REVIEW decision path for
applications tagged as high-impact (loan-decision, hiring-decision).

Article 25 — Data protection by design and by default
----------------------------------------------------------------
Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational
measures -- such as pseudonymisation -- designed to embed data protection
principles into processing from the outset ("by design"), and must
ensure that, by default, only personal data actually necessary for each
specific purpose is processed, in terms of the amount collected, the
extent of processing, the storage period, and accessibility. A governance
gateway that sits in front of every AI request is itself a natural
enforcement point for "by default" data minimisation -- for example,
redacting or withholding fields an application does not need for its
stated purpose before a response is returned, rather than relying on each
individual AI application to self-regulate what it surfaces.

Article 30 — Records of processing activities
----------------------------------------------------------------
Controllers (and processors) above certain thresholds, or engaged in
higher-risk processing, must maintain a written record of processing
activities: purposes, categories of data subjects and personal data,
categories of recipients, any international transfers, retention
schedules, and a general description of security measures. An AI
governance audit log that captures, for every governed request, the
application, department, data classification, decision, and matched
policy rule is functionally a live, continuously-updated Article 30
record for the AI systems it covers -- a byproduct of good governance
logging rather than a separate compliance exercise.

Article 32 — Security of processing
----------------------------------------------------------------
Controllers and processors must implement technical and organizational
measures appropriate to the risk, including (as relevant) pseudonymisation
and encryption, the ability to ensure ongoing confidentiality, integrity,
availability, and resilience of processing systems, the ability to
restore availability after an incident, and a process for regularly
testing and evaluating the effectiveness of these measures. HMAC-based
fingerprinting of identifiers in an audit trail (rather than storing raw
identifiers) and encryption of data at rest and in transit are concrete,
recognizable instances of "appropriate technical measures" under this
article, not just general security hygiene.

Articles 33-34 — Personal data breach notification
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 33 requires a controller to notify the relevant supervisory
authority of a personal data breach without undue delay and, where
feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless the breach is
unlikely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Article
34 requires notifying the affected individuals directly, without undue
delay, where the breach is likely to result in a HIGH risk to their
rights and freedoms. For an AI governance platform, an incident in which
an AI application's response leaked PII it should not have surfaced --
particularly if the leak was not caught and blocked -- is exactly the
kind of event this notification clock is designed for, which is part of
why blocking or redacting a detected PII leak before it reaches the
requester, rather than only logging it afterward, materially changes the
organization's regulatory exposure.

Article 35 — Data protection impact assessment (DPIA)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Where a type of processing, in particular using new technologies, is
likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms, the
controller must carry out a DPIA before the processing begins -- assessing
necessity and proportionality, the risks involved, and the measures
envisaged to address them. Systematic and extensive automated decision-
making with legal or similarly significant effects, and large-scale
processing of special category data, are both explicitly called out as
triggering circumstances. Deploying a new AI application into a
high-impact use case (loan decisions, hiring, health-adjacent support)
is a natural trigger point for a DPIA, and the governance policy
configuration for that application (risk tolerance, required review
paths, data classification handling) is a natural input to, and partial
record of, that assessment.

Chapter V — International transfers
----------------------------------------------------------------
Personal data may only be transferred outside the European Economic Area
where the destination ensures an adequate level of protection -- either
through a formal European Commission adequacy decision for that country,
or through appropriate safeguards such as Standard Contractual Clauses or
Binding Corporate Rules. For an AI governance system, this matters most
concretely when the underlying foundation model is consumed via an
API hosted outside the EEA: the data-flow question is not only "is this
request authorized" but "where does this request, and any logged context
derived from it, actually leave to," which is a distinct dimension from
the content-based risk checks (PII, bias, hallucination) most of this
system's other controls focus on.

Articles 82-83 — Liability and administrative fines
----------------------------------------------------------------
Article 82 gives any person who has suffered material or non-material
damage as a result of a GDPR infringement the right to compensation from
the controller or processor involved. Article 83 sets out administrative
fines on a two-tier structure: up to EUR 10 million or 2% of total
worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher) for certain categories of
infringement, and up to EUR 20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual
turnover (whichever is higher) for the more serious categories, including
violations of the core processing principles in Article 5, the lawful-
basis requirements of Article 6, and data subjects' rights. These figures
are the regulatory backdrop that makes "the AI response should not
surface unauthorized personal data" a board-level financial risk
question, not only a product-quality one.
