HIPAA — Prototype Healthcare Privacy Corpus (Summary Form)
============================================================
NOTE: Plain-language summary of HIPAA's core concepts for Policy RAG
demo/retrieval purposes, not verbatim regulatory text (45 CFR Parts 160
and 164). Not legal advice.

Protected Health Information (PHI)
----------------------------------------------------------------
HIPAA's Privacy Rule protects "individually identifiable health
information" held by a covered entity or its business associates --
information relating to a person's past, present, or future physical or
mental health condition, the provision of health care to them, or payment
for that care, when it identifies (or could reasonably identify) the
individual. This includes obvious fields like diagnoses and prescriptions,
but also identifiers such as names, dates, and account numbers when
combined with health context.

Covered entities and business associates
----------------------------------------------------------------
HIPAA's obligations apply directly to "covered entities" -- health plans,
healthcare clearinghouses, and healthcare providers who transmit health
information electronically in connection with certain transactions -- and
extend to their "business associates," meaning any person or organization
that performs a function involving the use or disclosure of PHI on a
covered entity's behalf (billing services, cloud hosting, and
increasingly, AI/analytics vendors). A business associate must operate
under a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with the covered entity,
contractually obligating it to safeguard PHI to the same standard.
Most general-purpose enterprise AI copilots are not themselves covered
entities, but if such a system processes PHI on behalf of a healthcare
organization that is a covered entity, the vendor operating it becomes a
business associate and inherits the same compliance obligations --
including, critically, that the underlying foundation-model API provider
may itself need a BAA in place before health-adjacent prompts can be sent
to it at all.

Minimum necessary standard
----------------------------------------------------------------
Covered entities must make reasonable efforts to limit use, disclosure,
and requests for PHI to the minimum necessary to accomplish the intended
purpose. This is the healthcare-specific analogue of GDPR's data
minimisation principle (Article 5) and is directly relevant to any AI
system that could retrieve health-adjacent information in response to a
broad query -- the system should surface only what the specific,
legitimate purpose requires, not the full record.

Permitted disclosures without authorization
----------------------------------------------------------------
PHI may generally be used or disclosed without specific patient
authorization for treatment, payment, and healthcare operations, and in a
limited set of other circumstances (e.g. required by law, public health
activities). Disclosure for purposes outside these categories -- for
example, an internal AI assistant surfacing a colleague's health
information to someone without a treatment, payment, or operations reason
to see it -- would fall outside permitted use and should be treated as an
unauthorized-access case, structurally identical to how ControlPlane
already treats unauthorized salary or financial-record access.

The Security Rule — administrative, physical, and technical safeguards
----------------------------------------------------------------
Distinct from the Privacy Rule's use-and-disclosure focus, the Security
Rule specifically governs electronic PHI (ePHI) and requires three
categories of safeguard. Administrative safeguards include a designated
security official, workforce training, and periodic risk assessments.
Physical safeguards cover facility access controls and device/media
disposal. Technical safeguards -- the category most relevant to an AI
governance platform -- require access controls limiting ePHI access to
authorized users (role-based access is the standard mechanism), audit
controls that record and examine activity in systems containing ePHI,
integrity controls to prevent improper alteration or destruction, and
transmission security to guard against unauthorized access to ePHI in
transit, typically through encryption. An audit trail that logs every
governed request touching health-classified data, with tamper-evident
fingerprinting, is a direct instance of the Security Rule's audit-controls
requirement, not a general best practice adopted independently of it.

Breach Notification Rule
----------------------------------------------------------------
Following a breach of unsecured PHI, a covered entity must notify
affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days
after discovery, and must notify the Department of Health and Human
Services -- immediately for breaches affecting 500 or more individuals,
or on an annual log basis for smaller breaches. Business associates must
notify the covered entity of a breach they discover, again without
unreasonable delay. An AI response that surfaces PHI to an unauthorized
requester, if not caught and blocked before the response reaches the
requester, can itself constitute a reportable breach depending on the
risk assessment required under this rule -- which is a direct argument
for blocking or redacting a detected health-data exposure pre-response,
the same logic that applies to the GDPR Article 33/34 breach-notification
clock for PII more broadly.

Patient rights — access, amendment, and accounting of disclosures
----------------------------------------------------------------
Individuals have the right to inspect and obtain a copy of their own PHI
held in a covered entity's "designated record set" (generally within 30
days of request), to request amendment of PHI they believe is inaccurate
or incomplete, and to receive an accounting of certain disclosures made
of their PHI over the preceding six years. As with GDPR's Article 15
right of access, this creates an operational requirement that health-
adjacent records -- including any logged AI interactions that reference a
specific patient or employee's health information -- be retrievable by
subject identity, not only by request or session ID.

Why this matters for a general-purpose enterprise copilot
----------------------------------------------------------------
Most enterprise AI copilots are not built for clinical use and are not
"covered entities" in the formal HIPAA sense, but an internal support or
HR assistant can still be asked questions that touch employee
health-related information (medical leave reasons, accommodation
requests, insurance claims). Treating any health-adjacent request as
high-sensitivity by default -- rather than only responding to explicit
"HIPAA" framing -- is the safer default posture, and mirrors GDPR Article
9's treatment of health data as a special category requiring a specific
justification rather than ordinary-course access.

Enforcement and penalties
----------------------------------------------------------------
HIPAA is enforced primarily by the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR),
which can impose civil monetary penalties on a four-tier structure based
on the covered entity's or business associate's level of culpability --
ranging from cases where the entity did not know and could not reasonably
have known of the violation, up to cases of wilful neglect that are not
corrected, with per-violation-category annual caps reaching into the
millions of dollars. Criminal penalties, prosecuted by the Department of
Justice, apply to knowing violations and can include imprisonment,
particularly where PHI is obtained or disclosed under false pretenses or
with intent to sell or use it for commercial advantage or malicious harm.
As with GDPR's fine structure, the practical effect for an enterprise is
that "did the AI system disclose health information it shouldn't have"
is a question with direct financial and, in the more serious cases,
personal-liability consequences -- not only a reputational one.
